# A diary

*A letter from May Askew, aged 51, widow and landlady, Holborn, London, to her sister Edie, dated 16 September 1940 and kept with her diary.*

**Monday 16 September 1940**

My Edie,

I have to apologise twice over. First, you must excuse the writing; time seems to pass extremely fast these days. Secondly, you must understand that all I did was because I miss the people I love: you, Mother and A.

It was Monday when I woke. I asked twice at the Royal Free, and the nurse said Mr Lucas had brought me in at about six in the morning. So I asked where M was. She had other people to see to. Of course she had. I could not stop feeling the shame, lying there watching her go from one bed to the next. At a time like this death is a burden on other people. Somehow, whenever she came near, I got ready to ask again.

Such a short way from the hospital to home, and it felt for ever. Passing the square, someone in the crowd told me that the bomb had gone off at last, as though I had been waiting for news of it. At thirty-seven the windows were broken. Glass on the pavement. I looked up at all those empty panes and thought of the people who would have to come back and find their things. Then I remembered my own key. It was in my pocket. I kept feeling for it all the rest of the way.

Nothing looked different when I opened the door, except that every corner had been cleaned, not a speck of dust. I stood in the passage listening. I took my coat off. A bunch of flowers on the table. They had told me they were going to shelter. I knew that, Edie. The two upstairs had said the Tube. I cannot blame them for going somewhere safe.

But when I woke burning hot and called out and there wasn’t anybody, it reminded me of the time, remember? The time I knocked at every door to see if anyone could give us something for the pain. I kept waiting for a door to open. Not even the boots over my head. All the noise outside, and inside I could hear myself moving about, every little thing I touched. I wanted somebody to be cross with me for waking them. Tull would have done. I would have listened to her for hours.

I broke the blue china Arthur brought me. I remember that much. You know which one. I have described it often enough in these letters, as though you might come and see it. I cannot put down what happened after. It was meant to be the last thing I had to do.

I have never asked to be born, nor to be saved. Forgive me, my dearest dear. I have been trying to make it only the fever ever since I woke.

There is nobody I can say this to. They would sit very carefully and look at me. I would have to watch their faces while I said it. With you I can get as far as the words, at least.

All that time waiting for Arthur. Isn’t that dreadful? All those journeys, and me here seeing to everything, and then he was gone altogether and I am supposed to remember how happy we were.

I tell people he died because it is the shortest word and they stop asking. He went, Edie. There is a difference, and I seem to be the only one who minds it. A man can go off with a case and his coat over his arm and everybody says how sad, and the woman left behind gets the bowls to dust. He never once asked whether I wanted to go anywhere. I would have said no. I should have liked to be asked.

You had cold feet too. Everybody I ever shared a bed with had cold feet, and nobody has ever warmed mine. Do you remember the winter you could not lie down? I sat you up against me till it got light, with the kettle going and the towel over both our heads so you could breathe the steam. You said it smelled of pennies. I counted to keep you breathing. One, two, three, Edie, breathe. The doctor would not come without his half-crown first, and Mother’s ring went for it, and he said what I knew already and put the half-crown in his waistcoat. I was out at work by then. I used to run home at dinner-time to see whether you were still sitting up.

There is a thing I have never told you, and since you never answer back I shall tell you now. One night you called me. I heard you. I lay still and told myself you would stop. I was so tired, Edie. I wanted one night that was mine, and you did stop, and I went to sleep. That is all. I am not going to say which night it was. On Thursday I called out in this house and nobody came, and I lay there listening to myself, and I thought, so this is what it is. This is what I gave you. I have had it coming a long while.

The doctor asked me twice whether it had been an accident. The second time I said yes. He wrote something down and did not read it out to me, and that was the end of it. They tell me I talked. I asked what about, and the nurse said nothing that made sense, which I have decided to believe. Somebody had brought my coat and shoes in, with the key in the pocket. I did not ask who. I am to go back on Friday for them to look at the stitches. The bandage catches the pen, which is why the writing is so bad, that and the rest.

Somebody has done the stairs. There is not a mark on the carpet, not a trace of the grey he used to bring in on his knees. The good cups are back in the wrong cupboard. The flowers are Michaelmas daisies out of somebody’s garden, in the brown jug, and there is no card, so I am not to know, am I. Tull would not spend money on flowers and Pike would not think of it.

I have not been up. His door is shut; it is always shut. I stood at the bottom of the stairs with my hand on the banister and I did not go up, and I am not going to, and you may make of that what you like. I shall know tonight whether there is a light. He must have carried me down the front steps, Edie, and I do not remember one of them. That is the part I mind most. Not the other.

Fenn has put a paper through every door. There is to be a meeting of the street on Tuesday week, in the parish hall, about the shelters and the fire party, and everybody is to come. I cannot go, Edie. Pike opens at six. The whole street will have seen me go out of my own front door in my nightdress, in the lodger’s arms, and now they are to sit in rows and look at the back of my head. Tull has been twice already and I did not answer the door. I shall have to, tomorrow.

You used to stand behind me and push, do you remember, when I would not go in anywhere. Both hands in the small of my back, and you so little. Do that. I shall sit here on Tuesday week with my hat on and wait for it.

I shall put this with the others.

Your May
