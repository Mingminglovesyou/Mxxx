# A diary

*Extract from the diary of May Askew, aged 51, widow and landlady, Holborn, London, 12 September 1940.*

**Thursday 12 September 1940**

The guns woke me from a moist dream. I was moss, stranded in no-man’s-land. Passed out altogether last night, after the tea, while writing in this diary. My nightdress stuck between my shoulders. Too much blanket, then none at all and my teeth going. Fever. The flame was cutting into every inch of my skin.

The blue china A brought back from Hong Kong several years ago. I have never seen so delicate a shape, such workmanship. Hold it to the light and you can see the thin layer of pattern. All those years with A away, it kept me company through the nights. I must have gone for it. I cannot remember picking it up.

Called out once. Nobody answered. All those rooms, and not a cough, not somebody dropping a boot just as I was getting off to sleep. Usually I could hear everything. The swirling sky, coughing in the opium smoke like an old clown, slowly burnt into an overexposed celluloid image. Edie gone within the frame. Later, silence. I stood by the hall window, at the edge of the curtain, with the cup in both hands. Between the acts, the aeroplanes cut through the canvas like a knife. There it is, the darkest shadow, which would absorb every word of colour coming to my throat. Not a figure, but a tree, in the fracture of time.

There isn’t a fig, only the infinite melancholy, with mineral oil dividing the sea.

He had asked if there was a fig. Before the rent, before anything sensible. Had I said there wasn’t room? No, that there wasn’t one. I could hear myself saying it, quite certain. No, there wasn’t. Now a branch reached towards the window. There was room enough for its shadow.

Another flash and the branches went through the mortar. I waited for the leaves to come back. Nothing. Only brick, which I had been looking at for years.

Fear washes over me. Must be the Tube. Of course. The two upstairs had gone down to the Tube. They had told me. Under the ground with their boots on, and I had been listening for them over my head. Mustn’t laugh, it hurt my throat. All that way down to get a night’s sleep. The cup wanted putting down. I ought to sit down myself. In a minute. When the shaking stopped.

Blue china against my palms. Cool still, on the side I had not been holding. I turned it. Cold feet, Arthur, always those cold feet, and all this heat going to waste. There would be water left in the kettle. Someone ought to—

A little light upstairs, where M’s room was.

*I hope I find you well, Mrs Askew.*

No. Not very. I could say that. But that is not his or her voice. Every fibre of my being is screaming, dragging me towards that siren; all I can do is hold my blue china, tighter and tighter. Carefully, so the water wouldn’t spill.

It is a luxury, to be immersed in this solitude. Whatever happened outside, however long the light stayed on, DO NOT GO.

they don’t believe in the same god as we do.
